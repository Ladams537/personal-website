A little white door—confessional—
opens to you, shoulders tensed,
in that green dress tight as a held breath,
silk clinging where my fingers hadn’t reached yet.
I was dead already. We hadn’t even been fed.

You leant over the flowers on the table,
inhaled slowly, eyes half-shut,
the way someone practises being seen—
and something ancient stirred—
the soft give of overripe fruit,
the slick stone floor of a place
where people go to be forgiven.

Serious with a smear of sacrilege.
Principled with a streak of sweat.
I gripped your hips, drew you into me.
Your hands mapped the territory
You had already claimed.

Every morning I left a reason at your door—
one thing I loved, slid under the armour
like a note beneath a locked gate.
Some days I think you read them.
Most days I think you held them
without opening your fist.
  
We both had a covenant between us—
clean hands until the ceremony—
but covenants were written by people
who’d never stood this close to you.

Star-gazing was a euphemism.
Your eyes were the only sky.
We watched light leave the room
the way conviction leaves a body
when the body wants something more
than conviction.

Frankincense. Myrrh. Your neck.
My T-shirt hanging off your shoulder
like a white flag.
I surrendered. Completely.

I did not move. Could not.
You climbed above me,
held my gaze like a lantern,
hips swaying, a metronome
ticking down our promises.
The Red Sea had opinions about restraint.
We let it speak.

Then morning: a serpent in the room,
coiled around blame, hissing
that every fear had landed.
Yours: that you’d given yourself to someone
who’d leave once he had what he wanted.
Mine: that I’d never reach the person
behind the person in the room.
We said the unsayable.
Built a coffin from the same wood
we’d meant to build a bed.

A year of knowing you
and I’d never seen a single friend,
never seen a photo from before
you learned to hide.
You gave me your body before your history—
and I wonder now which one
was the greater act of trust.
  
If we had undressed our defences
before we undressed each other—
would the ending have been gentler?
Or just slower?

Every ending is a crucifixion.
Every beginning, a rolled stone.
But resurrection doesn’t give you
a replacement heart—
just the same one,
with new scars to memorise.

Rewind. Translate. Sift.
Greek to Hebrew.
I’m an archaeologist of us,
brushing dust off fragments
that might be holy
or might be ordinary clay.

I said: Take the leap. 
You said: "I love you. But—"

That but is a canyon I've been 
Standing at the edge of ever since.

I went looking for proof I wasn’t mad—
sat across from women who opened easy,
showed me childhood photos over coffee,
introduced me to their friends on a first date.
And all it proved
was that the door I wanted
was the only one that wouldn’t open—
Hope.

Safety isn't the gospel.

They wrote about a man from Nazareth.
Nothing special to look at, they said.
Split families down the middle.
Ate with outcasts, drank with sinners,
flipped tables in sacred buildings,
and when they nailed him up for it
the sky went dark at noon.
  
If that’s what obedience to your own heart looks like,
then darling, disobedience to the world
is the most sacred act I know.

Here’s my counter-offer:
burn the manual. Shred the vows.
Write something so heretical 
the theologians sweat.

Have so much fun the seraphim 
blush and look away, 
and when the world ends at midnight, 
turn the hourglass over
and commit the same sins again.

I spent months after you
in the wilderness.
Forty days, give or take.
Learning that the opposite of love
isn’t hate, or indifference—
it’s a closed door
you still leave flowers outside of.

Forget the world.
Forget the flood.
I’d bleed daily for the privilege
of standing next to you.
The spirit says yes.
The flesh says please.

I’d trade forever for
one gasp of breath beside you,
a lifetime of safety
for your sweet, perfumed destruction.
To live one day inside eternity,
or eternity inside one day—
I’d have the Red Sea
take me all over again.
