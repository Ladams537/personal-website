“You came to me to learn the Pleasure of Life and the Pleasure of Art. Perhaps I am chosen to teach you something much more wonderful, the meaning of Sorrow and its beauty.” – Oscar Wilde

All of human wisdom is at our fingertips, yet even with all the answers, we often have no idea what to do. Spiralling, on the other hand, comes naturally. We face-plant on the ocean bed, and by the time we reach the bottom, we feel there is no one more deserving of this depth than us. We are chained by the expectations of a world that will not remember us, held captive by an identity it gave us. This perceived self becomes more important than reality itself.

The ocean bed, however, is a strangely comforting place. Stripped of everything, you have two options: quit or try anything.

Often, it takes a tragedy to shock us out of our routine, to stop the relentless inner narration. For a brief moment, the junk we’ve added to reality falls away, and we see the chains we forged for ourselves. This is the moment of clarity we so often waste. We wake up only to cuff ourselves again, becoming passengers in our own lives, filling the silence with distractions to avoid the boredom where the answers we seek lie waiting. Distracted by sirens. Our purpose becomes an afterthought. Desperate to please. Our childhood wounds covered with Play-Doh. On this foundation we try and build a house.

But a true tragedy sifts the soul. It separates the wheat from the chaff as light wrestles with dark on a knife's edge. The victor, as in any great battle, is not revealed until the final round. More battles are lost by a loss of hope than a loss of blood. And it is the bloodiest of battles that are won by the smallest of margins.

In this sorrow, there is beauty, for behind it lies a love that was shared and a life that was lived. What you choose to feed in this moment, you make stronger.

Most of life is not the pivotal, tragic moments, but the small ones in between—the practice before the performance. The days we remember forever are not when we are training, but when we are truly playing; when we lose our self-conscious narration and connect completely with reality.

So what if it played out differently? The question is pointless. You played. You gave the best shot you could with the practice you had. Perhaps you were underprepared. Give yourself some slack. The good news is that at any moment, you can choose to begin again: to face what you bury, to confront the self-imposed chains, and to choose life. You might just surprise yourself with the one you are capable of living.
