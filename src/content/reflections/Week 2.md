Søren Kierkegaard wrote that “Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards,” and this past week felt like a perfect, chaotic proof of that idea. It was a rollercoaster. For the first time in four years, I celebrated Anish's birthday—a moment of pure, forward-living joy. I even got drunk for the first time, another leap into the unknown (followed by a hangover that provided some painful, backward-facing clarity). The week was a blur of highs and lows, of sharp focus and heavy fog, a reminder that life doesn’t wait for us to make sense of it.

The challenge, of course, lies in the weight of looking back. I've wrestled recently with the urge to give up, to wallow in self-pity and blame the world. A fantastic memory is both a blessing and a curse. It allows me to recall every little moment of joy, but it also forces me to remember every time I have fallen short, every mistake, every word I wish I could take back. This is the trap of 'understanding backwards'—if we linger there too long, we become paralyzed, unable to move forward at all.

This tension is a universal one, and the path forward is captured beautifully by two different thinkers:

"The real voyage of discovery consists not in seeking new landscapes, but in having new eyes." — Marcel Proust.

“You can't go back and change the beginning, but you can start where you are and change the ending.” – C.S. Lewis

So how does one learn to live forwards under the often all too crippling weight of the past? Not through grand, celebrated victories, but through the quiet, consistent walk towards them. I am as much a sucker for a grand gesture as the next hopeless romantic, but it is in the consistency of everyday actions where the foundation that withstands the storm rests. Champions are made in the mundane failures and the small steps only we see. This is where the rarest trait of all emerges: moral courage. Courage is not the absence of fear; it is being afraid and doing it anyway. The fear of repeating past mistakes never truly subsides, but living forward means learning to control it, one day at a time.

Perhaps nowhere is this courage tested more than in our willingness to be vulnerable. Opening up—letting others into our innermost fears and dreams—is a terrifying leap. The first time, it is often born of naivety, and some win the lottery. But after you've been hurt, after your openness has been used against you, the instinct is to retreat into a shell. Yet, the demons we hide from only grow larger in the dark. The real choice, the courageous one, comes when you decide to trust again. To open up in spite of knowing the risks, knowing how badly you can be hurt. This isn't naivety; it is the conscious, terrifying, and beautiful act of choosing to live forwards.
