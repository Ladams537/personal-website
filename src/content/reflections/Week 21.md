As I navigated the rush of central London this week, moving from my first week back at work to dinners featuring a global map of cuisines, then retreating to the quiet of my desk to write poetry—I noticed:

I am living the life I once dreamed of. More than that, I am doing it effortlessly.

Yet, as a species, we are never satisfied with "effortless."

The modern world is designed to keep us in a state of perpetual dependence: chasing the next upgrade, the next hit of dopamine, or the next social validation.

The peak evocative moment of my week came while watching the movie Hamnet. I experienced a nauseating push-pull: societies change fast, but humans change slow.

We are biologically built for an environment that no longer exists.

For the vast majority of human history, meaning was forced upon us through the narrow lens of survival.

Your choice of partner was limited to the few eligible souls in your village.

Sex had direct, life-altering consequences.

Your social status was obvious, etched into the manual labor you performed, and your path was largely set before you from the day of your birth.

This is not the world we inhabit in modern London.

Here, we are told we can be anything. We are invited to discard our social hierarchies and choose new ones.

We have decoupled sex from reproduction, leaving our bodies often confused by the lack of biological follow-through.

On a simple walk to the tube, we see more attractive, high-status "competitors" in a single afternoon than our ancestors saw in an entire lifetime.

We have the spare time to agonise over the meaning of life precisely because we aren't worried about starving before winter.

Amid such a dramatic shift, how are we to navigate it? How can we live a "good life" when the very definition of "good" has been untethered from survival?

My naive side sometimes wishes life could be rerun—that I could trial multiple versions of myself like a simulation and pick the salient path.

But the lack of a redo is what gives the movie its gravity.

Life has no rehearsal.

I have no desire to spend my later years pandering to reclaim a youth wasted trying to fit into spaces that never fit me.

I don’t have all the answers.

Neither did Shakespeare.

No one does.

I have no idea which specific actions will lead to the best outcomes in a world of infinite choice.

But I cannot hate the past versions of myself; those actions, however clumsy or confused, made me who I am.

And I certainly cannot let a group of people, who are just as biologically bewildered as I am, dictate how I ought to live.

I am learning to trust my gut—that ancient, pre-linguistic compass that predates our modern anxieties.

I am learning to let my brain jam and process its static in the background, but I refuse to let the apocalyptic visions it casts up when I voyage into the unknown prevent me from being me.

In a world that offers everything, the bravest thing we can do is choose something, commit to it, and stop looking at the exits.

Perhaps the good life isn’t about making the right choice, but about being present enough to experience the choice we’ve already made.
