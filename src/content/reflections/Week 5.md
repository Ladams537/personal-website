title: "Gutters and Stars"
subtitle: "Week 5"

I’ve always carried the quiet conviction that I am special, a main character. Today I don’t know if this is cliché, laudable, or embarrassing to admit. It’s an internal engine of confidence, a belief that things will eventually work out in my favour regardless of my actions—the very root of many a day procrastinated away. Yet, the evidence argues otherwise. There are piles of missed opportunities, of harsh words, of forgiving too easily. In short, piles of failure. Within these failures, however, lie the hard-won lessons on which I now try to balance my life, the principles on which I try to understand myself.

This reveals a paradox: we are wired to avoid suffering, yet suffering is what works. It is the furnace that crystallises truth and burns away delusion. These truths are often so fundamental we don't even know we know them, the blind spots that form the very axis of our world. As Oscar Wilde said, “We are all in the gutter, but some of us are looking at the stars.” Suffering, it seems, is the gutter that forces us to look up. It teaches us to see a different world from the one we knew as a child.

This personal evolution is often at odds with the world's expectations. There is immense pressure to measure life by external validation, a path Tolstoy lamented in his final years: "It is as if I had been going downhill while I imagined I was going up. I was going up in public opinion, but to the same extent life was ebbing away from me." This is the core choice: to follow the truth forged in your own 'gutter', or to follow the crowd uphill towards an empty victory.

The latter path is a kind of collective madness. As Nietzsche wrote, “Madness is something rare in individuals — but in groups, parties, peoples, and ages, it is the rule.” He himself was a testament to this, selling barely a hundred copies of a book in his lifetime, only to become one of the most influential thinkers of the next century. He chose the rare madness of the individual over the rule of the group. And then his own thoughts were plucked and used for madness by people he would have been ashamed to call followers.

This brings me to a question that has sat with me all week, borrowed from Dead Poets Society: "What verse will you contribute?" The film suggests a beautiful idea: it is okay to write a simple poem, for the most beautiful are often about simple things. The only crime is to make it ordinary. The ordinary verse is Tolstoy's hollow climb; it’s our lust to be accepted by those we do not even like. The simple, beautiful verse is found by looking at the stars from your own, unique gutter.

Ultimately, what are we dedicating our lives to? Love? Harmony? Honour? These are ideas more powerful than any one person. Yet, no combination of words can truly capture them. Experience transcends language.

Perhaps, then, the goal isn't to write a verse at all. It is to choose to live it.
