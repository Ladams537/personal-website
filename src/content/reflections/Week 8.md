"To see a world in a grain of sand and a heaven in a wild flower, hold infinity in the palm of your hand and eternity in an hour." — William Blake

To know one rose is a revelation; to see a field of them is merely a view. The first encounter holds a certain magic, a crystalline finish to every moment. There is a grace in naivety. You cherish this first rose, for you have seen nothing like it. Then, one day, you learn it is a common flower—beautiful, yes, but not unique. In that knowledge, something precious is lost. The challenge, then, is to see each rose, again and again, as if for the first time.

This is the gift that children possess, the reason they so often inspire awe. They move through the world with an unblemished curiosity, finding delight in moments we adults have learned to take for granted. When the magic fades, when that first rose is lost, the tragedy is acute, but a child’s spirit does not stumble for long. It integrates the sorrow and simply finds a new playground with a new flower, allowing loss to fuel an ever-heightening sense of amazement for the journey ahead.

To carry that spirit into adulthood is not to avoid pain, but to face it without closing your heart. It brings to mind what Leonard Cohen wrote: "There is a crack in everything, that's how the light gets in." This truth is at the core of a story a friend once shared: Two men arrive in heaven. The first says, “Look, Father, I protected my heart. I never let myself get hurt. It is clean and pure.” The other says, “Father, I failed. I stumbled over and over. My heart is covered in scars.” Then Christ reveals His own heart, and it is just like the latter’s, for He loved everyone, and each one broke it, and He only allowed this to deepen His love for the next soul.

This parable has always resonated with me, perhaps because I have never cared for the hierarchies humans build to protect themselves. I retreated from the world as a child, only to re-emerge in university fascinated by those who lived by an internal compass. I found myself blind to tribe and convention, searching for souls who chose a scarred heart over an empty one—the people for who Henry David Thoreau characterised so elegantly by saying, "If a man does not keep pace with his companions, perhaps it is because he hears a different drummer. Let him step to the music which he hears, however measured or far away."

Living this way requires a certain speed, an urgency born from the perception that no moment should be wasted. This is an impossible standard, of course, but it is the pursuit that yields fruit. It is an internal itch that insists, “Do not delay. You know what matters. Forget convention, forget opinions, forget your own self-consciousness. Just follow the voice that calls to you.” This pursuit fosters an adaptability, a drive to solve the problems that matter to them, not to anyone else. That is their territory. That is where they are most alive.

This inner voice, when given space to operate, is the closest I have found to Truth. It is the echo of that first rose, a guide back to wonder. Following it is how one earns what Carl Jung called "the privilege of a lifetime... to become who you truly are." And it is one hell of an adventure.
