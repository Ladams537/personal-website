"So much of what people call 'conviction' is actually a wilful disregard for facts that might change their minds." — Morgan Housel

As we move through life, our former selves become strangers. Challenges that once crippled us now seem trivial; convictions that once burned brightly now appear faded under a new light. This constant embarrassment with who we were isn't a sign of failure, but the surest evidence of growth. We are, by nature, a first draft, always in motion.

But what fuels this restless cycle? In the silence, our thoughts often roar, accusing us of all we could be. We project this inner turmoil outward, becoming so consumed by what others might think that we forget to live. This is the source of the "quiet desperation" Henry David Thoreau described that so many men live: a life chained not by force, but by the perceived expectations of others, all while avoiding the real work of self-inquiry.

The first step toward freedom is a quiet rebellion. It is a conscious choice to stop serving ideas we no longer believe in, a sentiment captured perfectly by James Joyce:
"I will not serve that in which I no longer believe, whether it call itself my home, my fatherland, or my church..."

This rebellion isn't waged with anger but with acceptance. As Carl Jung said, "The most terrifying thing is to accept oneself completely." We often run from responsibility not because we fear others, but because we cannot bear our own shame. We cast ourselves as judge, jury, and executioner in our own story, inflating our importance to a preposterous degree. This self-judgment is an illusion of control born from pride—the belief that we are best positioned to deliver the final verdict.

"The more one judges, the less one loves," wrote Honoré de Balzac. Compassion frees us from compulsive shame far more effectively than criticism ever could. This reveals a central paradox: the relentless drive to improve can become its own trap, often fuelled by a deep feeling of inadequacy, a perpetual falling short of the ideal self we have curated for ourselves—a goalpost that moves each time you take a shot at it.

The constant striving begs the question: why not simply be happy with what is? As Socrates suggested, the secret lies in a radical shift of focus:
"The secret of happiness...is not found in seeking more, but in developing the capacity to enjoy less."

Then again, perhaps the ultimate aim isn't even happiness, but something deeper—a selfless love, or agape. But to fixate on any destination, even a noble one, is to miss the point entirely.

There is no final destination in this process. The absence of an answer is the answer. It frees us from the impossible burden of getting it right and instead gives us the permission to simply be. The only task is to choose, again and again, to begin.
