"The world breaks everyone," Hemingway wrote, "and afterward many are strong at the broken places."

The hero and the villain often share the same traumatic origin; the difference lies in the response. One says, “The world is cruel, I must be crueler.” The other, “The world is cruel, I must change it.”

Yet, our desire to change the world often masks a more insidious desire to control it. As I stood at a rooftop bar in Bulgaria this week, I realised how easy it is to let perspective justify action.

I thought again of Nietzsche, "whoever fights monsters should see to it that in the process he does not become a monster."

I played with both sides this week. I noticed a convenient cognitive dissonance: when I did good, I said, “I chose this.” When I did bad, I said, “This chose me.”

The monster I created to protect myself as a kid is difficult to manage, but it is as much a part of me as the dazzling side.

I gained insight into how this duality is perceived. A colleague confessed that upon our first meeting, he pegged me as a "cocky liar," citing my confidence, crazy adventures, and lack of patience for small talk.

It was only throughout the trip that he realised his impression was misplaced.

Emerson might comfort me by saying, "To be great is to be misunderstood," but the feedback stung enough to force reflection.

Perhaps he wasn't entirely wrong about the danger, though. This tension surfaced with a woman on the trip. I have learned that my intensity can be a drug for others—an escape—offering a brief, vivid high.

But without the capacity to be fully seen or truthful right now, allowing this connection feels more like negligence than love.

I took the DISC assessment through work and retook the Big 5 personality test to process this.

For the first time it dawned on me as I answered the questions: I am not easy to get to know.

I tell people of my wild adventures, sometimes even intimate details of my life—all attempting to shift the conversation away from the weather—but I rarely tell them who I am

All of the pieces are there, but I never give them the key to decode the cipher.

"For the best," I concluded. "A 48V circuit should not be connected directly to a 3.3V one." Sparks fly, certainly, but the hardware always gets fried.

To choose life fully—to run at high speed, to have Kerouac’s "mad ones" as ride-or-die friends—there is a delicately balanced cost: to be truly seen by few.

Bulgaria was beautiful. Despite the setbacks, I managed to balance the gifts with the costs.
