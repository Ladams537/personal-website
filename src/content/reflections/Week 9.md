A life spent asking "Who am I?" is a beautiful burden. As Bob Dylan put it, “I change during the course of a day. I wake and I’m one person, and when I go to sleep I know for certain I’m somebody else.” We are fluid—feathers floating in a hurricane—and this constant change brings a natural state of fear. If the man is different from one moment to the next, then what, exactly, is lost?

Rainer Maria Rilke wrote, "Perhaps all the dragons in our lives are princesses who are only waiting to see us act, just once, with beauty and courage." This feels true. Just beneath the surface of the man is a little boy, one who is wounded and seeking only to be told, “You are worth it.”

When that boy is wounded, we lash out. We reach for strength, hate, and rage—powerful forces that, like any drug, have effects that depend entirely on the dose. Overuse quickly erodes the soul. In my least desirable moments, these ooze out of me. It echoes Carl Jung: "Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate."

We’re all fascinated in our own way by tales of conflict. For good reason. In the face of trying obstacles, when the stakes are higher than many can fathom, the best and worst of humanity is bared naked. It is here we see the choice: some choose what is right, and some choose what is easy.

Perhaps the goal, then, is not to be a "good man," but an honest one. Romans 5:7 notes that "one will hardly die for a righteous man, though perhaps for the good man someone would dare even to die." But a scene from Equalizer III is perhaps more telling. The main character, a complicated lifelong assassin, is saved by a doctor. When he asks why, the doctor replies, in essence, "Because you did not claim to be a good man."

Honesty in the face of our own dragons is the first step. A tiny, yet monumental step. This has been a week of learning what comes next: forgiveness. First, the forgiveness of others. And then, the far more difficult task, the one that requires the most courage: forgiveness of the past self.

It is as Lewis B. Smedes wrote: "To forgive is to set a prisoner free and discover that the prisoner was you."
