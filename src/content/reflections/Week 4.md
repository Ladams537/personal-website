A TfL worker was wishing everyone good morning, and the simplicity of his attempt to spread joy struck me. It felt like a small act of defiance against a world that insists joy is only permitted when they deem the moment significant enough.

This week, my thoughts have turned to rebirth—the process of life, death, and the delicate balance between them. I can allow myself to become a prisoner of thought, captured by everything except the present moment. I can wait for permission to act, wait for the perfect moment, only to realize no such time will come. Living each day is a choice. We are masters at squandering the present.

"It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it... We are always complaining that our days are few, and acting as if there would be no end of them." — Seneca

This waste is fuelled by endless narration. Many of us spend our lives viewing the world from ground level, convinced that our constant commentary about what others must think of us is reality. It is nothing but a veil we use to mask our self-obsession. As Eckhart Tolle observed, "The moment you become aware of the ego in you, it is strictly speaking no longer the ego... Ego implies unawareness. Awareness and ego cannot coexist."

Without this awareness, we remain possessed by our narratives. We blame the world for our circumstances, passing responsibility to fate with subtle excuses: "This is who I am. This is who I have always been. This is who I will always be." We carve our story in stone before it has even begun. But life is not a static monument—it is motion. Anaïs Nin wrote, "Life is a process of becoming, a combination of states we have to go through. Where people fail is that they wish to elect a state and remain in it. This is a kind of death."

When we embrace that static death, we write a boring story. I am young, yet I am already struck by how years compress into sequences of moments, how they drift away and become nothing but sensations of a time that once was. Perhaps we are measuring the whole thing incorrectly. As Maya Angelou said, "Life is not measured by the number of breaths we take, but by the moments that take our breath away."

We beat ourselves up for not reaching the mountaintop. In the gym, I am confronted by delusions I can no longer uphold. In the mirror, I no longer see the boy who was afraid of the world, who sought refuge in food and video games. He had many dreams, most of which have been surpassed. Yet I still allow myself to be robbed of the simple pleasures my life affords me. My gratitude proves transient. My hunger for life wanes not when I feel bad, but when I forget to notice the beauty in the mundane.

Be more like the TfL guy.
