"History, despite its wrenching pain, cannot be unlived, but if faced with courage, need not be lived again." - Maya Angelou

This week marked Remembrance Day, a time to recall those who made permanent, sacrificial expeditions. That same thought struck me deeply during a walk around Oxford, where the weight of history is inescapable. Every cobblestone and college wall whispers of ancestors and ideas that shaped us.

We forget so quickly. In our modern rush, we become fickle spirits (or maybe we always have been), forgetting not only our history but also our future-selves.

Alexander Solzhenitsyn remarked that “the line of good and evil runs through the heart of every man.” His quiet witness, born in the gulags, arguably did more to topple an empire than many armies. His pen, fuelled by conviction, was a powerful sword.

That line runs through my own heart. There have been times this year I have been fickle, where I've hurt myself and, worse, hurt others. I see now that this had little to do with willpower and everything to do with the convictions—or lack thereof—underlying my perspective.

There was no time this was clearer than while I was building a bed with my friend. I kept placing components the wrong way. This simple, frustrating act felt like a powerful metaphor. In those seasons I hurt others, it wasn't that I chose the wrong direction; it was that I refused to choose one at all.

Flirting with different paths but committing to none, I built a life that reflected my internal state: an ugly mixture of all my heart’s desires. Unwilling to choose a path, my path was chosen for me. Seneca said, "If one does not know to which port one is sailing, no wind is favourable."

This has become even clearer through my recent coding. I can build applications all day, but with no clear goal, I end up with disconnected knowledge—good for a theory exam, but useless in reality. It's the illusion of being busy, “always learning and never able to come to a knowledge of the truth.”

A brutal quote I read this week nails this idea: "There is nothing so useless as doing efficiently that which should not be done at all." - Peter Drucker

A person must first acknowledge they are lost in order to be found. This, I believe, is the "courage" Maya Angelou spoke of—the courage to face the history of my own fickleness. Only then can I, and all of us in our own unique story, stop building disconnected parts, and finally, with conviction, choose a port and sail.
