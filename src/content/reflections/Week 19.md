Travel has a way of stripping back the layers of the everyday. While an itinerary offers safety, there is a specific, raw power in choosing to have none. Without a map, you don't just see a city; you see the world's bare metal.

Walking through Istanbul, you see the corporate conquest. Instead of nations taking land, businesses take the street corners. You see Burger King, McDonald’s, and Starbucks—the same logos, the same interfaces, just housed in different architecture.

It’s a city where Christian, Muslim, and modern histories collide. The streets are a chaotic blur of smoke and total disregard for traffic laws. Yet, beneath the noise, you realise that humanity operates on the same hardware, even if our operating systems are worlds apart.

Different city names; the same human insides.

Abandoning a plan invites a certain childish curiosity. It clears the cache of your pre-installed apps—habit and expectation—leaving only the core version of yourself.

This state is as terrifying as it is powerful. In these moments of focused reflection, you have the agency to either heal a year of hurt or double down on a mistake. You see the world for what it is:I am leaving Istanbul with the smell of smoke in my coat and a perspective on my own identity.

I’ve realised that adopting a few well-known logos—the standard ways we communicate and present ourselves—doesn’t mean I’ve failed to stand out. It’s a necessary interface. It’s how I help others navigate the complexity of who I am.

The reboot was messy, but the machine is handling more load than ever. As I move into 2026, I’m looking for the streets that challenge my hardware—streets that are unpredictable, loud, and alive—and the ones that force me to see this beautiful, complicated world exactly as it is.
- The frantic comedy of a tourist taking eight hundred photos of their partner, only to be told they are all insufficient
- The quiet grace of a stray cat claiming a lap in a crowded cafe
- The weight of history in the Hagia Sophia and the Basilica Cistern, forcing you to ask the big questions as we step into 2026

As I looked over the Istanbul skyline, I realised that I, too, am a convergence of the universal and the unique. I share the same music, buildings, and emotions as the crowd, but my upbringing and the choices I’ve made over the last two decades are my unique advantage. They are the filters that help me navigate the noise. They are the gut feelings that whisper True North when the world gets loud.

2025 was a year of drastic software changes. I walked many streets and met many versions of myself. Under pressure, I saw my bugs—the impatience when things move slowly, the aimless drift of long exploration. These aren’t errors; they are features that come bundled with my strengths.

I am leaving Istanbul with the smell of smoke in my coat and a perspective on my own identity.

I’ve realised that adopting a few well-known logos—the standard ways we communicate and present ourselves—doesn’t mean I’ve failed to stand out. It’s a necessary interface. It’s how I help others navigate the complexity of who I am.

The reboot was messy, but the machine is handling more load than ever. As I move into 2026, I’m looking for the streets that challenge my hardware—streets that are unpredictable, loud, and alive—and the ones that force me to see this beautiful, complicated world exactly as it is.
