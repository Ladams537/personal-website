---
title: "Regret as a Compass"
date: 2026-03-23
excerpt: "A straps class, a mirror, and what regret is actually for."
---

I wanted to reminisce about my aerial silk days that I used to do with a friend. So I locked a straps session in at the same place. Arrived fashionably late, as I do. Thirteen women, one other guy (the instructor). Not used to this. I felt out-of-place, a tad uncomfortable. Stretched: physically and mentally. Then the session kicked off.

Shoulder movement -- fine; twirling around -- embarrassing, but honestly fun; swinging -- I was hopeless! The women before me had waltzed through the air, effortless, free looking. I stood on the chair, hopes high, and performed a series of near-perfect front-planches. The instructor, taken off guard, but impressed, says, "No no Louis, swing." I replied, "I'm trying. I can't. My body won't do it." He laughed, a little dismissive, came over and pushed me hoping that momentum would carry the rest of it.

Nope.

I would thrust forward, a fencer trying to score a point on my opponent, when the whole time I was meant to be a dancer.

My grip strong, until it was not. All these muscles, and yet when tasked with something any beginner could do on instinct, my body — impressive as it looked — could not do it.

Shame -- projected by myself onto myself. I tried to play it cool in front of the room, have a laugh about it. Inside, I was angry, humiliated, and kind of sad.

I left knowing something had to change.

Leading up to this straps class, I'd stopped looking at myself properly.

Not in the way people mean when they talk about self-image. I still trained. I still looked the part. But somewhere between the fourth and fifth month of the same programme, I'd stopped checking whether the body in the mirror was someone I recognised or just someone I maintained.

There was an evening — coming out of the shower, towel round my waist, passing the mirror without stopping — where I caught my reflection and paused. Not because something was wrong. Because nothing was. Everything was exactly where I'd put it. And I felt nothing.

A physique I'd spent years building, and the sight of it produced the same response as seeing my own front door. Familiar. Functional. Inanimate.

My brain had become superb at simulating risk. Every scenario played out three moves ahead: the rejection, the embarrassment, the confirmation that I was not as capable as the body suggested. And those simulations were usually right — most of the time, putting yourself out there does feel clumsy, does look foolish.

What the simulation couldn't model was the other side. The feeling of being alive again. The same circuitry that protected me from looking like an idiot had no mechanism for calculating what it felt like to _want_ something again.

At twenty-two, the simulations run fast and loud. The body has plenty of time to recover from the things the mind is terrified of.

Post-straps I decided to start stretching and doing zone-two cardio again. Initially, I was struggling. Not just physically, but mentally. I could feel the shadow of my former self staring back from the gym mirrors: _Really? You're stretching? What happened to you big man?_

Sweaty, carrying a little more body fat than I'd like, still taking hits from other areas of my life -- what appeared to be a young guy stretching was a quiet rebellion against everything he'd built.

It's uncomfortable having multiple personas: traditional, rebel, angry, sensualist, purist, and more -- too many to name.

As I get older I see: all have their place in my life.

All are a part of me.

But no single one should hold the whip over the others for too long or else I become stiff, predictable, unable to deal with the multi-faceted demands of being alive.

When I trained only for strength, my body forgot how to swing. When I let only the cautious voice speak, I forgot what it felt like to want something. The pattern is the same. A default left unrotated doesn't just stagnate — it anaesthetises. The field doesn't burn. It greys.

And then one day a random straps class hands you a mirror and you see what the monoculture has cost.

The same circuitry which kept me on the sidelines now terrorises me from the other direction: _"You realise how much you left on the table? How long you sat there? Embarrassing."_

Regret shapeshifts. First it locked the door. Then it kicked the door open. Now I'm learning to use it as a compass — the regret I will feel from inaction overpowering the simulated regret of acting now and looking the fool.

I'm wearing a different persona now. I'm not sure when this block of training ends.

Maybe it's when progress stalls and the body asks for something new again. Maybe it's when I feel that same greyness and recognise it this time. I don't know.

I'm mid-season. The crop is in the ground. I don't know the yield yet.
